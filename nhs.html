Context

Cancer prehabilitation, preparing patients physically, nutritionally and psychologically for treatment, was already established across the NHS sites in this programme, but engagement and delivery varied considerably between patients and clinical settings. Some patients never engaged with the service at all, and staff across sites reported inconsistent ways of introducing it. Understanding why required looking at both sides of the interaction: what patients experienced when offered the service, and how clinical teams delivered it day to day.

Research Questions

  • What prevents patients from accessing or engaging with prehabilitation services?
  • How do patients experience communication and introduction to the service?
  • What operational and capacity barriers affect how clinicians deliver it?

Study Design

1
Multi-Site Coordination and Stakeholder Engagement
Process
Research was coordinated across multiple NHS sites delivering the prehabilitation programme, working with clinicians, service leads, patients and public contributors to plan data collection around live clinical schedules and governance requirements at each site.
Why multiple sites?
Delivery already varied noticeably between sites. A single-site study would not have distinguished barriers common to all patients from those created by local operational context, staffing, or service maturity.
2
Clinical Observations and Focus Groups
Process
40+ structured observations and focus groups were conducted with clinical and operational staff across sites, examining how the service was introduced, scheduled and delivered in practice.
Why direct observation?
Staff do not always report workflow friction unprompted, particularly workarounds that have become routine. Direct observation surfaced operational and communication issues that interviews alone would likely have missed.
3
In-Depth Patient Interviews
Process
10+ in-depth interviews were conducted with patients, including those from diverse and underserved backgrounds, exploring their expectations, communication experiences and accessibility barriers across the cancer care pathway.
Why patient interviews specifically?
Observation captures what happens inside the clinical encounter, but not what shapes a patient's decision to engage before or between appointments. Interviews were the only route to understanding expectations, hesitation and accessibility barriers from the patient's own perspective, especially for underserved groups least likely to be captured through routine service data.
4
Thematic Analysis and Synthesis
Process
Observation, focus group and interview data were coded and analysed thematically in NVivo, with codes cross-referenced across data sources into a combined theme map before being synthesised into a set of findings and recommendations.
Why triangulate across methods?
Analysing all three data sources against a shared coding framework, rather than reporting them separately, made it possible to check whether a theme reflected a genuine pattern across perspectives or was an artefact of a single method or site.

Key Insights

1
Accessibility barriers, not clinical suitability, were a primary reason some patients did not engage, and these barriers fell disproportionately on diverse and underserved patient groups.
2
Communication gaps around what prehabilitation involved and why it mattered often arose early in the pathway, sometimes before a patient had decided whether to attend at all.
3
The timing of when the service was introduced relative to a patient's diagnosis and treatment planning strongly shaped whether they engaged with it.
4
Operational and capacity constraints at some sites limited how consistently staff could introduce and support the service, independent of patient-level factors.

Impact

Findings were synthesised into recommendations that informed service redesign, staff training content and patient-facing engagement materials. These were shared with NHS programme leads and site teams to support more consistent delivery and more accessible communication about the service across sites.
